Doomsday Was Today

2026-11-13

Today is Friday, 13 November 2026. In 1960, three engineers at the University of Illinois named this day doomsday. Their paper in Science had a striking title, “Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026.” On this date, they wrote, world population would become infinite if it kept growing as it had for the last two thousand years.

It is also the birthday of the lead author, Heinz von Foerster, born on 13 November 1911.

The world did not end this morning. About 8.3 billion people woke up as usual. But the paper is worth a second look. It was wrong in an interesting way. And the same reasoning, run in the opposite direction, gives an answer that fits our time better.

How they got the date

The authors collected 24 estimates of world population, from AD 0 to 1958. Then they asked which curve fits these points best.

The usual answer in population science is a curve that slows down as numbers grow, because resources run short. Von Foerster and his colleagues proposed the opposite. Humans talk to each other, work together and learn to control their surroundings. So the more people there are, they argued, the faster the population grows. Such a curve never levels off. It shoots up to infinity on a fixed date. Their data put that date in mid-November 2026.

Their fitted curve was

N=1.79×1011(2026.87−t)0.99,N = \frac{1.79 \times 10^{11}}{(2026.87 - t)^{0.99}},

where NN is world population and tt is the year. As tt approaches 2026.87, the bottom of the fraction shrinks to zero and NN grows without limit. Because the power is close to 1, population roughly doubles each time the years left to doomsday halve.

Can we get the same date today?

Almost exactly. The paper does not list its numbers, but its figure names the source of each point. I went back to those old almanacs and reference books and fitted the same curve. The date comes out within a month of theirs.

But our picture of the past has changed since 1960. Today’s best estimate for AD 0 is about 230 million people, more than twice the 100 million used in 1960. With today’s estimates, the same method puts doomsday in 1997.

Adding the real population counts since 1960 does not help either. Each new decade of data pushes doomsday further away. Since 1990, it has always stayed about 30 years ahead of the latest data. It moves away as we approach it.

What the curve missed

World population growth peaked in 1963, three years after the paper appeared. It has slowed ever since.

A demographer saw this coming. In 1961, Ansley Coale of Princeton wrote to Science. Population was growing fast, he said, only because fewer people were dying. Birth rates were not rising. And everywhere, the things von Foerster praised (education, communication, control over nature) had led people to have fewer children, not more.

Coale was right. Demographers call this the demographic transition. Death rates fall first, and birth rates follow. It started in Europe around 1800 and has since reached almost every country. Today more than half of the world’s countries have fewer than 2.1 children per woman, the level at which each generation replaces itself. About two thirds of the world’s people live in these countries.

The last human

So here is the reverse question. The 1960 curve ran a past trend forward to infinity. What happens if we run today’s lowest fertility forward to one person?

South Korea has one of the lowest fertility rates in the world, 0.80 children per woman in 2025. If the whole world had this rate forever, each generation would be about 40 per cent the size of the one before. Population would halve about every 24 years. Starting from 8.3 billion people today, the world would reach its last human in 2826. Counting the days, as the 1960 authors did, gives Friday, 29 May 2826.

The calculation is short. Each generation is RR times the size of the one before. RR is the net reproduction rate, about the total fertility rate divided by 2.1, so R=0.80/2.1=0.38R = 0.80/2.1 = 0.38 for Korea. A generation lasts TT years, the mean age of mothers at childbirth, 33.8 years in Korea. Starting from N0N_0 people, the number of generations until one person is left is ln⁡N0/ln⁡(1/R)\ln\,N_0 / \ln\,(1/R). So the years until the last human are

Tln⁡N0ln⁡(1/R)=33.8×ln⁡(8.3×109)ln⁡(2.1/0.80)=33.8×22.840.965≈800.T \, \frac{\ln\,N_0}{\ln\,(1/R)} = 33.8 \times \frac{\ln\,(8.3 \times 10^9)}{\ln\,(2.1/0.80)} = 33.8 \times \frac{22.84}{0.965} \approx 800.

At the fertility of the European Union, the last human would come in the 3600s. At Türkiye’s fertility, in the 3700s. At today’s world average of about 2.25 children, population keeps growing and there is no last human at all.

Do not believe this date either

This date is as fragile as the one from 1960. It assumes that today’s rate will hold forever. It will not. Korea’s fertility rose from 0.72 in 2023 to 0.80 in 2025. That small rise alone pushed the end of the species back by almost 80 years.

Demographers separate a projection, which says what would follow if certain assumptions held, from a forecast, which says what we actually expect. Both 2026 and 2826 are projections. The news stories this month read the 1960 date as a forecast that failed. The last human is not a forecast either.
